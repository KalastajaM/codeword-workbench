How the solver fills a codeword grid

Every playable cell holds a number, and every number stands for one letter. The solver turns the grid into word slots, finds words that fit each slot, and searches for an assignment where all crossing words and number mappings agree. This page shows the stages, then lets you step through a real search.

3 · The other two strategies

When the word list is not enough

Missing-word branch and bound

Printed puzzles contain names and brands no list covers. Here every slot has an extra option, “no listed word”, which costs one. Search keeps a cap on that cost and widens or tightens it in phases.

  • Starts with a cap of one unsupported word. With no hypothesis after a node slice, the cap grows to max(cap + 1, ⌊1.5 × cap⌋).
  • Each improvement restarts from the root with a cap one below the best cost found.
  • With no hypothesis by the last quarter of the budget, an uncapped dive guarantees some answer.
  • Words are tried least-constraining first: letters that many crossing candidates share.

Dictionary-free annealing

Without a word list, the solver optimises the number-to-letter mapping itself. A move gives one number a new letter; under unique letters the number that held it swaps. Only slots touching the changed numbers are rescored.

  • Temperature falls linearly from 5.3 to 0.3 over each 1,200-step run; a worse move is accepted with probability eΔ/T.
  • Each hard-rule violation (forbidden pattern, ruled-out letter, rejected word) costs 1,000 points, so they are repaired, never returned.
  • Runs restart with a shuffled alphabet and a fixed seed, keeping the 12 best distinct mappings.
4 · Limits

Search budgets

The clock starts after preparation (loading, model adaptation, indexing). Any limit that cuts the search marks the result as non-exhaustive.

ModeTimeSearch nodes
Quick4 s30,000
Deep60 s1,000,000
Extended300 s5,000,000

Fixed in every mode: 500 ranked candidates per slot in strict search, 20,000 kept for missing-word search, 20,000 fragments per open slot, 12 alternatives, and a queue of about 200,000 cells of pending states (for example 2,469 states on a 9 × 9 grid). A search that finds nothing is not proof the puzzle is impossible.
